Section A: Spiritual Progression Portfolio Expansion

Faith, Agency, and Technical Stewardship

In Information Technology, technical skill without moral character is incomplete. True professional excellence requires aligning daily engineering decisions with enduring spiritual principles. Reflecting on Doctrine and Covenants 58:26–28 reinforces that exercising righteous agency means taking ownership of outcomes rather than doing the bare minimum (The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, 1981).

Integrity and Data Stewardship

Whether configuring a PostgreSQL database or securing a Linux server, engineers are entrusted with systems that hold real people's information and livelihoods. Applying strict access controls, encryption, and honest documentation is an expression of spiritual integrity, protecting what has been entrusted to my care even when no one is auditing my work.

Courageous Problem-Solving (Be Not Afraid, Only Believe)

Navigating complex cloud deployments or unfamiliar Linux kernel behaviors often tests patience and confidence. Drawing on President Jeffrey R. Holland’s teaching to choose faith over fear when facing uncertainty helps me approach difficult technical obstacles with calm persistence, testing hypotheses one step at a time until a clean solution emerges (Holland, 2021).

Grace Under Pressure (Come What May, and Love It)

Technical environments inevitably produce unexpected errors, tight deadlines, and team friction. Elder Joseph B. Wirthlin’s message on responding to adversity with resilience and charity guides how I collaborate with peers, focusing on shared solutions rather than blame (Wirthlin, 2008).

Section D: Deep Project Refinement & Ethical Analysis (Flagship Project)

Focusing on Project 3: Linux Server Performance Tuning & Security Hardening

Complex Technical Challenges & Refinements Implemented

1. Challenge 1: Balancing Strict Security with Service Availability

  • Decision & Refinement: Enabling SELinux in enforcing mode and stripping broad root privileges initially blocked legitimate web and deployment workflows. Rather than disabling SELinux or granting blanket ALL=(ALL:ALL) ALL sudo rights, I audited denial logs, restored proper file security contexts (restorecon), and authored scoped drop-in rules in /etc/sudoers.d/ allowing service accounts to restart only specific systemd units (Red Hat, 2025).
  • Impact: Eliminated privilege-escalation pathways while maintaining zero-downtime service deployments.

2. Challenge 2: Preventing Silent Resource Exhaustion Under Load

  • Decision & Refinement: High-concurrency workloads risked exhausting file descriptors and memory swap space. I established baseline telemetry using iostat and df -i, then methodically adjusted vm.swappiness and fs.file-max one parameter at a time via /etc/sysctl.d/, documenting each change against latency benchmarks (Red Hat, 2025).
  • Impact: Reduced disk I/O bottlenecks and improved system responsiveness under peak load.

Ethical Considerations & Decision-Making Documentation

  • Privacy vs. Surveillance in Log Monitoring: Centralized logging (journald and fail2ban) is essential for intrusion detection and forensics, yet excessive logging can inadvertently capture sensitive user payloads or personal identifiers. I configured log rotation and restricted log read permissions strictly to audited administrative accounts, so security monitoring protects users without violating their privacy (National Institute of Standards and Technology [NIST], 2020).
  • Accountability Over Convenience: Eliminating shared root logins in favor of individual accounts with sudo auditing creates a transparent record of administrative actions. Ethically, true accountability protects both the organization and honest administrators by ensuring every change is traceable and reversible (NIST, 2020).
